Nate's Expansion to Nation Domination

Nate has 6 in-person clients in LA, deep training expertise, little spare time and no audience. This plan packages that expertise into online products that add $3,000 a month, using channels that work without followers.

Assigned by Jacob Peterson · Research. Checked Oct 30, 2026. Prices link to their sources. Every client count, price point and conversion below is labeled as an assumption for planning, not a forecast.

SummaryBottom line

1Don't sell workouts. Sell Nate's judgment, at a premium, to a few buyers

$3,000 a month doesn't need an audience. It needs roughly 12 premium online clients, or 8 gyms or trainers licensing his programming, or a mix of the two. Both are small enough to fill through referrals, local B2B outreach and a few paid listings. Cheap mass products (ebooks, $19 PDFs) need traffic Nate doesn't have, so in this plan they're add-ons, not the engine.

Top 3: (1) a premium hybrid/async coaching tier on Everfit or Trainerize, (2) licensing his programming to trainers, small gyms and physical therapy clinics, and (3) one expert specialty program sold through Stan or Whop and advertised on Google search. The fixed tool stack runs about $50–$150 a month, plus insurance that covers online training (from $159 a year).

0What Nate has, and what that rules out

0.1Assets: expertise, 6 clients who already trust him, an LA location. Constraints: little time, no audience

Strategies that depend on going viral or posting daily are out. Nate's edge is that he knows training deeply. That shows up best in work buyers can judge directly: a program a gym owner can run, a coaching check-in that catches a bad squat pattern, or a niche plan that clearly comes from someone who has done this before. Every idea below is scored on setup hours, weekly hours once running, and whether it needs an audience.

Planning assumption: Nate can give it about 10 hours a week for the first 6 weeks, then 4–6 hours a week. Change the math if that's wrong.

1Ways to package the expertise (ranked by fit, not by size)

1.1Premium hybrid/async coaching (the core engine)

How it works: clients get a custom program in the app, film their key lifts, and send a weekly check-in form. Nate replies with video notes and adjusts the plan, with no live sessions. Offer an expert tier such as "form-review coaching from an LA trainer" or a niche like strength for adults over 40, post-PT return to lifting, or hybrid athletes.

Tools and prices: Everfit Starter is free for up to 5 clients, and Pro runs $19/mo for 5, $29 for 10 and $49 for 20, plus Payments & Packages from $9 and Autoflow automation from $29. Everfit Pro includes an "AI Smart Response" feature for messages. Trainerize Pro is $25/mo for 5 clients and $50 for 15, plus Business ($25) and Stripe ($10) add-ons. TrueCoach Standard (20 clients) is $57.99/mo billed annually, with no transaction fee beyond Stripe's.

Time: 15–20 hours to set up (templates, intake form, check-in form, welcome video). Ongoing, about 15–20 minutes per client per week (assumption). That's roughly 3–4 hours a week for 12 clients.

Math (assumptions): $249/mo × 12 clients = $2,988 gross. Everfit Pro at 20 clients ($49) plus Payments ($9) plus Stripe-style processing of about 3% (about $90) leaves about $2,840 net. You get to 12 clients through the referral engine (2.4) and the PT and gym partners (1.3). Nothing here depends on followers.

1.2License Nate's programming to other trainers and small gyms (sell the brain, not the hours)

How it works: each month Nate writes one block of programming, such as a 4-week strength-and-conditioning cycle with exercise videos and coaching cues. Gyms, bootcamps and solo trainers pay a monthly license to run it with their own members. One block serves every licensee, so the work doesn't grow with the number of buyers. Everfit Studio and Trainerize Studio are built for distributing programs to teams and members, but a shared app or Google Drive folder works at the start.

Who buys: independent LA gyms without a head coach, new trainers who don't want to program, apartment-building fitness rooms (sold through property managers), and corporate wellness programs. Find them through cold email and LinkedIn, Google Maps lists, and in person. None of this needs an audience.

Time: about 20 hours to build the first 3 blocks and a sample. Then about 6–8 hours a month (assumption), which barely changes as he adds licensees.

Math (assumptions): 4 gyms at $399/mo plus 6 solo trainers at $99/mo = $1,596 + $594 = $2,190/mo. Together with 4 premium coaching clients from 1.1 ($996), that clears $3K. Contract basics: a license, not a sale; no resale; Nate's name stays on it; buyers are responsible for supervising their own members.

1.3Physical therapist and chiropractor referral partnerships: "after PT, train with Nate"

How it works: PT patients are often discharged before they're strong. Nate offers clinics a defined post-discharge program, in person in LA or async online, with a progress report sent back to the clinic if the client agrees. It runs both ways: Nate refers clients with pain or medical issues to the PT. NSCA guidance already says trainers should keep a referral network and refer complex cases.

Time: 5 clinic visits or emails to start, then about 1 hour a month to maintain each relationship. Risk: Nate must not treat injuries. He takes the client after clearance and stays inside training. No paid referral fees to licensed providers without legal advice.

1.4One expert specialty program sold through Stan or Whop, with Google search ads

How it works: a single high-trust product in a niche Nate knows cold, e.g. "12-Week Strength Return for Lifters Over 40" or "Hybrid Athlete Base Block", priced at $99–$149. It comes as video exercise demos, a printable plan and an app version. Buyers find it through Google search ads on intent keywords like "strength program after 40", which put it in front of people already searching, with no audience needed. The checkout upsells the premium coaching tier.

Tools and prices: Stan Creator is $29/mo (course builder, subscriptions, lead magnets, booking). Creator Pro ($99/mo) adds email flows, upsells, discount codes and ad pixels. Stan takes no platform fee, but payment processing still applies. Whop has no monthly fee: domestic cards cost 2.7% + $0.30, with optional add-ons up to 6% + $0.30 total.

Time: 25–35 hours to film and build. About 1 hour a week after that.

Math (assumptions): 10 sales × $129 = $1,290/mo gross, before ad spend. We don't have ad costs or conversion rates for Nate's niche, so treat the first $300–$500 of ads as a test and only scale if the cost per sale stays under about 40% of the price. Spending this money needs Nate's approval.

1.5"Coach the coaches": paid mentorship and program reviews for newer trainers

How it works: new trainers pay Nate to review their client programs, critique filmed technique, or join a monthly group call. Sell it as a $49 program review, or a $149/mo mentorship for a small cohort of up to 8 trainers. The $149/mo tier pairs naturally with licensing (1.2). Fiverr can bring in buyers without an audience. Sellers keep 80% of each completed order, and funds clear in 14 days.

Math (assumptions): 6 mentees × $149 = $894/mo for about 2 hours a week. Fiverr program reviews: 10 × $49 × 80% = $392/mo.

1.6Templated meal-structure guides and done-for-you macro templates (inside California's rules)

How it works: sell general-nutrition meal-structure templates (high-protein grocery lists, macro calculator spreadsheets, meal-prep guides) as an add-on to coaching or on their own. Tools like Everfit's Meal Plans add-on (from $39/mo) or Trainerize Advanced Nutrition ($20/mo at Pro 5–15) can generate plans from a client's macros automatically.

The law: California BPC §2068 lets anyone give general nutrition advice. It gives no authority to diagnose, treat or cure any disease or condition. Anyone giving nutrition advice commercially must post a specific NOTICE at their place of business (at least 8.5×11 inches, with "NOTICE" in letters at least 1 inch tall). The titles "dietitian" and "RD" are protected. Rule for Nate: general and performance nutrition only. Refer anyone with diabetes, an eating disorder, kidney disease or a similar condition to a registered dietitian. Never say "dietitian" or "clinical". Put the §2068 notice on his site and in every product.

2Getting buyers without followers

2.1Thumbtack: local and online leads without followers

Thumbtack charges per lead. Trainers set their own lead price, or pay only when a customer responds to an "Opportunity". There's no published fixed price for trainer leads, so test it with a small weekly budget. Use it to fill premium online coaching slots and in-person upsells.

Thumbtack: paying for leads

2.2Etsy: printable programs and trackers (small add-on income)

Etsy charges $0.20 per listing (renewed every 4 months), a 6.5% transaction fee, and 3% + $0.25 payment processing in the US. Offsite Ads sales carry another 12–15%. On a $19 printable that leaves about $16.40 before ad fees. It's good for being found, and each file links to his coaching. It won't reach $3K on its own without heavy volume, so don't count on it.

2.3Amazon KDP: a book as a credibility tool

Ebook royalties are 70% of the list price minus delivery costs in eligible countries, or 35%. Paperbacks pay 60% of the list price minus printing costs (50% below $9.99). A short paperback with an "apply for coaching" QR code gives him authority and an occasional lead. Treat royalties as a bonus.

2.4Referral engine built on his 6 current clients

This is the strongest channel he has, and it costs nothing. (a) Offer each in-person client one free month of online coaching for a friend or family member who lives out of state. Their network is the audience. (b) Give a credit toward sessions for every referral that signs up. (c) Ask for a Google review after each milestone, since reviews help his Google Business Profile show up in local search. Assumption: if 6 clients bring 1 qualified intro every 2 months, that's about 3 leads a month.

2.5AI-assisted check-ins: the time saver that keeps it under 5 hours a week

Use the app's automation (Everfit Autoflow, from $29/mo, and AI Smart Response, or Trainerize Business automations) to schedule forms, reminders and program rollovers. Nate can have an AI assistant draft check-in replies, but he reviews every reply before it goes out. Clients pay for his judgment, and AI mistakes on injuries are a liability.

3Risks and must-dos before selling anything

3.1Insurance, waivers, nutrition scope, subscriptions, taxes

Insurance that covers online training: Insurance Canopy from $159/yr (covers live and pre-recorded online classes). Insure Fitness Group $189/yr ($1M/$3M, live and pre-recorded). K&K from $179, but it excludes training material open to the general public. Sadler from $194 covers live virtual training only, not recordings. Pick a policy that covers recorded and async programs, because that's most of this plan.

Waivers and intake: a health questionnaire (PAR-Q style) and an online waiver for every buyer, which K&K specifically recommends for virtual training. Nutrition: follow the §2068 rules in 1.6. Subscriptions: California's Automatic Renewal Law (BPC §17600 and following) requires clear terms and easy cancellation for recurring billing. Check this with a lawyer before launch. Licensing contracts (1.2) and PT partnerships (1.3) should get a short legal review. Taxes: keep online income in a separate account and talk to an accountant.

4.290-day plan

Days 1–14: buy online-coverage insurance. Set up Everfit (free tier) or Trainerize. Write the intake form, waiver, check-in form and §2068 notice. Choose ONE niche. Offer 3 "founding" online spots at $199 to current clients' friends and family (2.4).

Days 15–45: build 3 licensable programming blocks and a one-page sample PDF. Contact 20 independent LA gyms, 10 PT clinics and 10 new trainers (cold email and walk-ins). Launch Thumbtack with a small weekly budget. Goal: 6 online clients and 2 licensees.

Days 46–90: film the specialty program and put it on Stan ($29) or Whop. Run a $300–$500 Google Ads test, with Nate's sign-off. Raise new online clients to $249. Turn on automations. Add a Fiverr program-review gig and 3 Etsy printables. Review on day 90: keep what pays per hour and drop what doesn't. Target: $3K/mo gross run rate.
